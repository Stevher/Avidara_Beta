Batch manufacturing records sit at the centre of every GMP inspection. They are the single documentary proof that a product was manufactured, tested, and released exactly as the approved process dictates. When SAHPRA or PIC/S inspectors walk onto a production floor, the batch record is almost always the first document they request - and the one most likely to generate findings that escalate into critical observations.

## What "Inspection-Ready" Actually Means

An inspection-ready batch record is not simply a completed template. It is a contemporaneous, unambiguous, fully traceable account of everything that happened during manufacture of a specific batch. PIC/S Guide PE 009 and SAHPRA's adoption of PIC/S GMP standards set clear expectations: every entry must be made at the time the action occurs, in indelible ink, with the identity of the person performing each step clearly recorded. Pre-printed instructions must correspond exactly to the current approved master formula and processing instructions.

Inspectors check for alignment between three layers: the registered dossier (Module 3.2.P.3 for drug products), the site master file, and the actual batch record in hand. Any discrepancy - a mixing time that differs from the validated range, a piece of equipment not listed in the registration dossier, an in-process control limit that has quietly shifted - is treated as evidence of an uncontrolled change. That alone can convert a minor observation into a major one.

## Common Deviation and CAPA Documentation Gaps

Deviations recorded in the batch record are expected. What triggers findings is how they are handled. Inspectors routinely look for three things: whether the deviation was documented at the time it occurred, whether a root cause investigation was completed to a sufficient depth, and whether the resulting CAPA was effective and closed within a reasonable timeframe.

The most frequent gap is the "shallow investigation." A yield falls outside specification, and the deviation report states "operator error" without any supporting evidence, interview record, or assessment of whether previous batches were similarly affected. PIC/S inspectors will trace the deviation number back to the site's CAPA register and check whether the corrective action actually prevented recurrence. If the same deviation category appears across multiple batches with near-identical root cause statements, the inspector sees a systemic quality culture problem rather than an isolated event.

Another persistent weakness is the failure to link the deviation to a formal impact assessment on released product. SAHPRA expects that any deviation with potential patient-safety impact triggers a documented risk evaluation - not a retrospective note added weeks later.

## How Validation Records Tie Back to the Batch Record

Process validation and batch records are not parallel systems; they are interdependent. Inspectors verify that critical process parameters (CPPs) and in-process controls documented in the batch record fall within the proven acceptable ranges established during process validation. If your validation protocol defines a granulation endpoint moisture of 1.5–3.0% and the batch record shows 3.4% with no deviation raised, the inspector has found both a process control failure and a documentation failure in a single line.

Cleaning validation records receive similar scrutiny. Inspectors cross-reference the equipment identifiers in the batch record against cleaning logs and validation protocols to confirm that the equipment train used was validated for the specific product and that hold times between cleaning and use were respected. Gaps here - a missing equipment logbook entry, an expired cleaning validation - are among the most common critical findings in SAHPRA facility inspections.

## Findings That Trigger Repeat Inspections

Certain categories of batch record findings carry disproportionate consequence. Data integrity failures - entries written in pencil, use of correction fluid, backdated signatures, or unsigned yield calculations - almost guarantee a repeat inspection and can lead to suspension of a manufacturing licence. PIC/S classifies these as critical because they undermine the reliability of every other record at the site.

Repeated observations across batches are equally damaging. If an inspector reviews five batch records and finds the same type of incomplete entry in each, the finding shifts from a documentation lapse to a quality system deficiency. SAHPRA has shown an increasing willingness to issue GMP non-compliance letters requiring a formal remediation plan with defined milestones before the next inspection is scheduled.

Failure to review and approve batch records before product release is another red flag. The Qualified Person (or Responsible Pharmacist in the South African context) must have documentary evidence that they reviewed every critical step, deviation, and analytical result before authorising release. A blanket signature without evidence of substantive review does not satisfy PIC/S expectations.
