Periodic Safety Update Reports remain one of the most scrutinised pharmacovigilance deliverables in any regulatory system, and South Africa is no exception. SAHPRA's expectations have tightened considerably as the authority aligns more closely with international standards while retaining distinctly local requirements. A PSUR that sails through the EMA or a stringent reference authority may still attract queries — or outright rejection — from SAHPRA if it does not address South African-specific considerations. Understanding exactly where global templates fall short is the difference between a smooth review cycle and months of back-and-forth correspondence.

## The Regulatory Framework: ICH E2C(R2) Meets SAHPRA's PV Guideline

SAHPRA's pharmacovigilance guideline explicitly references the ICH E2C(R2) format as the expected structure for PSURs. This means the familiar architecture applies: an executive summary, worldwide marketing authorisation status, estimated patient exposure, presentation of individual case histories, studies, benefit-risk evaluation, and a summary of safety concerns. However, referencing ICH E2C(R2) and fully satisfying SAHPRA's interpretation of it are different things.

SAHPRA expects the data lock point (DLP) and reporting interval to align with the approved South African registration. Where a product was registered in South Africa years after the international birth date, the authority may request a PSUR interval that does not match the global cycle. This misalignment catches many marketing authorisation holders off guard. If the South African DLP differs, the PSUR must either be produced on SAHPRA's cycle or supplemented with a bridging addendum that covers the gap period with locally relevant data.

## Common Gaps Between a Global PSUR and SAHPRA's Expectations

The most frequent deficiency SAHPRA reviewers flag is the absence of South African-specific data. A global PSUR that aggregates adverse drug reaction reports by region but buries South African cases within a broader "rest of world" or "Africa" category does not meet the mark. SAHPRA wants to see local case narratives called out, local spontaneous reporting volumes quantified, and any signals arising from the South African population discussed explicitly.

Exposure estimates present another gap. Global PSURs typically derive patient exposure from IMS or IQVIA data weighted toward developed markets. If the South African exposure denominator is estimated crudely — or worse, omitted — reviewers will query the report. Companies should use local sales data or units-distributed figures to generate a credible South African exposure estimate and present it alongside the global figure.

A third recurring issue is the handling of local labelling versus the Company Core Data Sheet (CCDS). The PSUR should clearly compare the approved South African Professional Information (PI) against the CCDS and flag any discrepancies. Where the local PI is narrower than the CCDS — for instance, missing a recently added warning — the PSUR must explicitly propose whether a PI update is warranted and provide a timeline.

## Benefit-Risk Narrative Pitfalls

The benefit-risk evaluation is where many PSURs lose their way. SAHPRA expects a structured, ICH-aligned benefit-risk framework — not a discursive essay restating clinical trial efficacy data. The evaluation should follow a logical sequence: characterisation of benefits in the approved indications, characterisation of risks based on cumulative safety data, assessment of the benefit-risk balance, and a clear conclusion with any risk minimisation actions.

One common pitfall is presenting the benefit argument using pivotal trial data alone without acknowledging post-marketing effectiveness signals or real-world evidence that may temper the original efficacy narrative. Another is minimising emerging risks by burying them in appendices rather than integrating them into the benefit-risk discussion. SAHPRA reviewers are trained to spot this, and a report that appears to downplay safety signals will attract detailed queries.

The narrative must also avoid generic language. Statements such as "the benefit-risk balance remains favourable" without product-specific, data-driven justification will not satisfy reviewers. Every assertion needs evidence tied to the data presented earlier in the report.

## Aligning the Risk Management Plan with the PSUR

SAHPRA's GVP-aligned expectations mean the Risk Management Plan (RMP) and the PSUR should be mutually reinforcing documents. The safety specification within the RMP — covering important identified risks, important potential risks, and missing information — must map directly onto the safety concerns discussed in the PSUR. If the PSUR identifies a new signal or reclassifies a potential risk as an identified risk, the RMP should be updated in parallel, and the PSUR should state that this update is planned or has been submitted.

Cross-referencing between the two documents should be explicit, not assumed. SAHPRA has rejected PSURs where new pharmacovigilance activities were proposed in the report but had no corresponding entry in the current or upcoming RMP. Treating the PSUR and RMP as isolated deliverables is a recipe for regulatory friction.
