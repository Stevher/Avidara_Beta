Every person who touches a dangerous goods package - from the warehouse packer who affixes a label to the driver who signs the transport document - must be trained, and that training must be documented. South African law is explicit on this point, yet non-compliance remains one of the most common findings during roadside inspections and depot audits. The consequences range from spot fines and vehicle impoundment to criminal liability when an untrained handler causes an incident that harms people or the environment.

## The Legal Framework

Two primary instruments govern dangerous goods training obligations on South African roads. The **National Road Traffic Act 93 of 1996** (NRTA) and its regulations - particularly the National Road Traffic Regulations of 2000 - set the overarching requirement that no person may consign, pack, load, or transport dangerous goods unless adequately trained and in possession of the prescribed permits. **SANS 10228** (the identification and classification of dangerous goods for transport by road) and **SANS 10229** (packaging) provide the technical standards that training must cover, while **SANS 10231** details operational requirements for the transport of dangerous goods by road, including explicit training provisions.

These standards are incorporated by reference into the NRTA regulations, giving them the force of law. Compliance is therefore not optional, nor is it a matter of best practice - it is a statutory requirement enforceable by traffic officers, dangerous goods inspectors, and the South African Police Service.

## Who Must Be Trained

The regulations cast a wide net. Training obligations apply to **consignors** (the entity offering goods for transport), **packers** (anyone who places dangerous goods into packaging or assembles packages into overpacks), **loaders** (those who place packages onto a vehicle), **drivers** (who must hold a valid dangerous goods permit - the PrDP category "D"), and **operators** (the transport company holding the operating licence). Emergency responders at depots and along transport routes have separate but overlapping training needs, typically addressed through SANS 10232-4 emergency information systems and employer-specific emergency response plans.

Each role requires training matched to function. A packer does not need the same depth of route-planning knowledge as a driver, but both must understand classification, labelling, segregation, and emergency procedures relevant to their tasks.

## What the Training Must Cover

SANS 10231 specifies that training programmes must address, at minimum: the nature of the hazards presented by the goods; classification and identification using SANS 10228 UN numbers and proper shipping names; marking, labelling, and placarding; packaging selection and performance standards under SANS 10229; documentation requirements (including the dangerous goods declaration/transport document); loading, segregation, and securing of packages on the vehicle; emergency response procedures including the use of SANS 10232 emergency information; and the legal obligations and penalties under the NRTA.

For drivers specifically, PrDP (D) training and testing - administered through accredited testing centres - covers vehicle inspections, route selection, parking restrictions, tunnels and built-up areas, and the driver's duties at the scene of an incident.

## Refresher Intervals and Record-Keeping

The regulations require refresher training at intervals not exceeding **two years** for all dangerous goods handlers. In practice, annual refresher sessions are advisable because regulatory updates, product changes, and staff turnover make two-year cycles risky. PrDP permits for drivers are renewed every two years, with a requirement to demonstrate continued competence.

Documentation is where many organisations fail inspections. An inspector will expect to see, for every trained individual: the person's name and identification number; the date of initial and most recent refresher training; the topics covered and the name/qualification of the trainer; evidence of competence assessment (test results or practical observation records); and copies of valid PrDP permits for drivers.

These records must be available at the operator's premises and, for drivers, a summary or card confirming training status should be carried with the transport documents on the vehicle.

## What an Inspector Will Check

During a roadside inspection or depot audit, inspectors typically verify three things simultaneously: the transport documentation, the physical condition and labelling of packages, and the competence of personnel. They will question the driver on emergency procedures, segregation rules, and the meaning of hazard placards. An inability to answer is treated as evidence of inadequate training - and the liability flows upstream to the operator and consignor.

## Structuring an In-House Programme

Organisations that handle dangerous goods regularly should build an internal training programme rather than relying solely on external providers. A sound programme includes a training needs analysis mapped to each role, a syllabus aligned to the SANS standards and NRTA regulations, competent trainers (either qualified in-house staff or contracted specialists), written assessments and practical evaluations with documented pass criteria, a training register maintained in an auditable format, and a calendar of refresher sessions linked to individual expiry dates.

Integrating DG training into the broader quality management system - rather than treating it as a standalone exercise - ensures it receives management review and continuous improvement.
