Every person who touches a dangerous goods consignment — from the warehouse packer who assembles an overpack to the driver who carries it on a public road — must be trained, and that training must be documented. South African law is unambiguous on this point, yet non-compliance remains one of the most frequent findings during roadside inspections and depot audits. The consequences range from spot fines and vehicle impoundment to criminal liability under the National Road Traffic Act (NRTA) when an untrained handler is involved in an incident.

## The Legal Framework

Training obligations for dangerous goods (DG) handlers sit at the intersection of several instruments. The NRTA (Act 93 of 1996) and its National Road Traffic Regulations (particularly Regulation 273 and the schedules that incorporate SANS 10228, SANS 10229, and SANS 10231) establish the baseline. SANS 10228 (Identification and classification of dangerous goods for transport) and SANS 10231 (Transport of dangerous goods — operational requirements) are the technical standards that give substance to the Act's broad mandates.

Under Regulation 273B, no person may offer dangerous goods for transport, and no operator may transport them, unless every individual involved in the handling chain has received training appropriate to their function. The regulation does not distinguish between a multinational logistics company and a one-truck operation — the obligation is identical.

## Who Must Be Trained

The regulations cast a wide net. Training requirements apply to at least four categories of personnel:

**Packers and loaders** must understand classification (SANS 10228), packaging specifications (SANS 10229), marking, labelling, and segregation rules. A packer who places a Class 3 flammable liquid in the same overpack as a Class 5.1 oxidiser has created a segregation breach that no amount of downstream paperwork can fix.

**Drivers** require the most comprehensive syllabus. Beyond classification and documentation, they must know vehicle placarding, emergency procedures, route restrictions, and the practical use of personal protective equipment (PPE). Drivers carrying goods above the exemption thresholds specified in SANS 10231 must hold a valid PrDP (Professional Driving Permit) endorsed for dangerous goods, issued under Regulation 115.

**Consignors and transport planners** — the people who prepare transport documents, select packaging, and determine routes — must be trained on documentation requirements, declaration obligations, and the legal consequences of misdeclaring a consignment.

**Emergency responders and on-site safety personnel** require scenario-based training covering spillage containment, fire response per hazard class, and the use of the emergency information prescribed in SANS 10232-3 (the HAZCHEM system).

## Documenting Compliance

Training that is not documented is, for regulatory purposes, training that never happened. Inspectors expect to see a training record for each individual that includes the date of training, the syllabus covered, the name and qualifications of the trainer, an assessment result, and the date of the next refresher.

SANS 10231 does not prescribe a single national certificate format, but the record must demonstrate that the content was matched to the person's actual function. A generic "dangerous goods awareness" certificate will not satisfy an inspector who finds a driver unable to explain the meaning of a placard on the vehicle he is operating.

Refresher training must occur at intervals not exceeding two years, and sooner if regulations change, the company's product profile changes, or an incident reveals a knowledge gap. The two-year cycle aligns with the ADR (European Agreement) model that SANS 10231 draws upon.

## What Inspectors Actually Check

During a roadside inspection, a traffic officer or dangerous goods inspector will typically verify that the driver can produce a valid PrDP with the DG endorsement, that transport documents match the cargo, and that the driver can explain the emergency procedure for the specific goods on the vehicle. At a depot or warehouse audit, inspectors look at the training register, cross-reference names against duty rosters, and confirm that refresher dates have not lapsed.

A finding of untrained personnel can trigger an immediate prohibition notice, preventing the consignment from moving until compliance is restored. Repeat findings invite prosecution under Section 89 of the NRTA, which carries fines and potential imprisonment.

## Structuring an In-House Programme

An effective in-house DG training programme starts with a role-based training matrix that maps each job function to the specific SANS standards and regulatory clauses relevant to that role. The programme should combine classroom instruction with practical exercises — tabletop emergency simulations, hands-on placarding, and documentation drills.

Use internal audits to test whether training translates into practice. Walk the warehouse floor and check whether segregation tables are being followed. Review a sample of transport documents for completeness. These operational checks close the loop between the training room and the loading bay.

Maintain a centralised training register, ideally digital, with automated alerts for upcoming refresher deadlines. When regulations are amended — as happens whenever SANS standards are revised to align with the UN Recommendations on the Transport of Dangerous Goods — update the syllabus and retrain affected personnel promptly.
